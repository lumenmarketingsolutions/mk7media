How to build an offer people actually want to buy

November 13, 2026 · 6 min read · MK7 Media

An offer people want to buy has five parts: a clear result the customer cares about, a specific package that delivers it, a price that feels fair next to what they get, something that lowers the risk of saying yes, and an honest reason to act now. When ads fail, the offer is very often the real problem, because even great ads cannot make people want something that feels vague, risky or overpriced.

Your offer is more than your product. Two shops can sell the same perfume at the same price, and one sells out while the other struggles, because one packaged it in a way that makes buying feel easy and obvious.

Here is how to build one, step by step.

Step 1: Start with the result your customer wants

Very few people want a gym membership for its own sake. What they want is to feel confident at a wedding in June. A business owner paying for a Shopify store is really paying for orders that come in while they sleep.

Write down, in your customer's own words, what they actually want. The best source is real conversations: WhatsApp chats, reviews, sales calls and the questions people ask before buying. Look for the phrases that come up again and again.

A useful exercise is to finish this sentence: "My customer wants to ______ without ______." For example: "My customer wants clear skin before her engagement without spending months trying random products."

Step 2: Package it into something specific

Vague offers are hard to buy. Compare these:

  • "Skincare consultations available"
  • "A 30 minute skin assessment, a personalized 3 step routine, and a follow up check in after 4 weeks"

The second one is easy to picture. The customer knows exactly what they get, so they can decide.

Make your package specific about:

  • What is included (items, sessions, features)
  • How long it takes or when they will see results
  • What happens next after they pay

Step 3: Price it against the value, not your costs

Most small businesses price by adding a margin on top of cost, then wonder why customers say it is expensive. Customers do not know your costs. They compare your price with what they get and with their other options.

A few ways to make your price feel right:

  • Show the value of each part. If a bundle includes items that would cost $90 separately, and the bundle is $65, say so.
  • Offer three options. A basic, a recommended and a premium version. Many people pick the middle one, and the premium option makes it look reasonable.
  • Compare to the real alternative. A $300 website maintenance plan feels different next to the cost of a site that goes down during a sale.

In Lebanon and the Gulf, quoting a clear price upfront, in the currency you actually charge, also builds trust. Many customers message "how much?" before anything else, so the answer should be ready, simple and confident.

Step 4: Lower the risk of saying yes

Every purchase has some fear behind it. Will it work? Will it fit? Is this shop real? Your job is to make that fear smaller.

Options that help:

  • Guarantees. "If it doesn't fit, exchange it within 7 days." Only promise what you can honor.
  • Cash on delivery for online orders, which many local customers trust more than paying in advance with a card.
  • Proof. Real customer photos, video testimonials, before and after results (where that is allowed for your industry), and how many customers you have served.
  • A smaller first step. A trial class, a sample size, a paid audit that is credited toward the full project.

Step 5: Give an honest reason to act now

If there is no reason to buy today, people put it off and forget. Urgency works, but only when it is real. Fake countdown timers and "only 2 left" on every product damage trust quickly.

Honest urgency looks like:

  • A seasonal deadline: "Order by December 15 for delivery before the holidays"
  • Limited capacity: "We take 8 new clients per month"
  • A launch price that genuinely ends on a set date
  • A bonus for the first group of buyers

Step 6: Add bonuses that solve the next problem

A good bonus removes the next obstacle the customer will face. Someone buying a coffee machine will need beans. Someone buying a website will need content. Someone buying a skincare routine will want to know the right order to apply products.

Bonuses that solve a real problem raise the perceived value far more than discounts do, and they protect your margins.

A worked example: rebuilding a weak offer

A hypothetical business, with example numbers, to show the process.

A gym in Beirut runs ads for "Monthly membership, $60." Ads get clicks, but few signups.

They rebuild the offer:

  1. Result: "Lose your first 5 kilos in 8 weeks with a coach who checks in on you."
  2. Package: 8 weeks of access, 2 coached sessions per week, a simple nutrition plan, and a weekly WhatsApp check in.
  3. Price: $199 for 8 weeks, compared with $120 for two months of plain membership plus $25 per personal session if bought separately.
  4. Risk reversal: "Attend at least 12 sessions, and if you don't see progress, your next month is free."
  5. Urgency: "Next group starts March 1. 15 spots."
  6. Bonus: a body composition scan at the start and end.

The ads now speak to a specific person with a specific goal. The price is higher than before, but it feels like better value because the customer can see exactly what they are getting. Each new customer is now worth $199 upfront instead of $60, so even if fewer people sign up per click, the gym can come out well ahead.

How to test your offer

You do not need to guess. Run the same ad creative with two different offers, keep everything else the same, and compare the cost per sale or cost per lead over a week or two. Or ask your best customers directly: "If we offered X, would you have bought faster?"

Once the offer works, everything else gets easier. Ads get cheaper, your website converts better, and your sales team has less convincing to do. Our paid ads work always starts with the offer for this reason.
